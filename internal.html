Internal SoWs

Most people picture a statement of work as a contract with an outside vendor. It does not have to be. Inside a company that runs many agents, the same document governs work one team's agent does for another team's, and the terms deserve to be just as explicit as they would be with a stranger.

Why write it down inside one company

Companies already write internal agreements. IT signs service levels with the business. Shared services bill departments through chargeback. Finance publishes a close calendar and expects everyone to hit it. Those agreements exist because "we are all on the same team" does not answer who pays, how much capacity anyone may use, or when the report is due. Agents make the same questions sharper, because agents act at machine speed and spend real money.

An internal Agent SoW answers them the same way an external one does:

  • Chargeback. The price clause meters internal cost allocation instead of a sale. Finance can see which department's agents consume what, from settlement records instead of estimates.
  • Capacity protection. The volume clause protects shared budgets: model spend, vendor API quotas, the platform team's infrastructure. One team's enthusiasm cannot starve everyone else.
  • Data boundaries. Compensation data, HR records, and deal financials have walls inside a company too. Sealed transport and retention limits are enforced; conduct promises are recorded and become compliance items.
  • Deadlines that exist. Interim deliverables give the month-end report an actual due date, and a missed date is detected and recorded, not remembered in a retro.
  • Changes a person signs off. Starting the engagement can be routine, and the agents can do it. Changing the rate is not routine, because a new rate moves money between two departments' budgets. The document says which is which: formation by agent, amendment by a person, so a rate change waits for the director's own confirmation instead of an agent approving it overnight.
  • Something for audit to read. The evidence chain (signed tasks, settlement records, delivery verdicts) is exactly what internal audit and controls teams ask for and rarely get.

Example: commissions close, Finance for Sales operations

Finance runs a ledger-close agent. Sales operations needs commissions reconciled every month and asks questions about mismatches all month long. The engagement makes the monthly report an obligation with a date, prices the work for chargeback, and protects close week with volume limits. It also says who is allowed to change the rate: the two agents may form the engagement between themselves, but an amendment waits for a person, because the rate is the number that moves money between two cost centers.

sow_c4f8n2k1p9r5t7w3 · internal · provider: Finance · client: Sales Ops active
parties     ledger-close (Finance) serving sales-ops (Sales Operations)   [EVIDENCE]
scope       commission-reconciliation, mismatch-explain                   [ENFORCED]
inputs      CRM commissions export, text/csv, per task                    [ENFORCED]
interim     commissions-report.pdf, monthly, due by the 3rd               [EVIDENCE]
price       1,200,000 XCR per reconciliation, charged back to Sales       [ENFORCED]
approval    formation by agent, amendment by a person                     [ENFORCED]
volume      4 per hour, 20 per day                                        [ENFORCED]
disputes    escalate_to_owners (the two directors read the record)        [EVIDENCE]
promises    comp data: no human reading outside a dispute                 [RECORDED]
The price clause is chargeback, not revenue. Same mechanics, different accounting: Finance's cost center gets credited, Sales Operations' gets debited, and both sides hold the receipts.

Example: lead enrichment, Data Platform for Marketing

The data platform team runs an enrichment agent backed by paid vendor APIs. Marketing's campaign agent sends leads to be enriched. The work is free internally, but the volume clause is the whole point: it protects a shared vendor budget from being spent by one campaign.

sow_m8d2q6v4x1z9b5j7 · internal · provider: Data Platform · client: Marketing active
parties     enrich (Data Platform) serving campaigns (Marketing)          [EVIDENCE]
scope       lead-enrichment                                               [ENFORCED]
inputs      lead list, text/csv, per task                                 [ENFORCED]
deliverables enriched-leads, application/json, per task                   [ENFORCED]
price       no charge (internal service); every task still recorded       [ENFORCED]
volume      500 leads per day                                             [ENFORCED]
term        quarterly, renewed with the budget cycle                      [ENFORCED]
disputes    none (it is free; a disagreement is a conversation)           [EVIDENCE]
No charge does not mean no record. Nothing is rated and no settlement record is produced, but every task is still recorded and attributed, so the platform team can show exactly who uses the service when budget season comes.

What changes inside one company, and what does not

The document is identical: same clauses, same three labels, same signing rule, same document states, and the same optional bookends around the work, a validation probe before signing and a review after delivery. What typically differs is the choices. Price becomes chargeback or no charge. The arrangement still has to be declared, and internal work is where time and materials earns its keep: an open-ended migration or investigation gets a rate schedule over metered units and a not-to-exceed cap, so the receiving department knows the most it can be charged back before it agrees, and the reserved amount is visible in its budget from the day the engagement forms. Disputes are usually escalate_to_owners, because refunds between cost centers are theater; the two managers reading the same signed record is the real remedy. Confidentiality promises carry more weight, not less: internally, a recorded promise is a policy commitment, and the evidence chain shows whether it held.

Standing proposals work internally too. A platform team publishes its standard terms once, and other departments countersign into them, which is what keeps an internal agent catalog accurate: the listing every team browses is generated from the terms they would be agreeing to. How that derivation works is the subject of the catalog page.
